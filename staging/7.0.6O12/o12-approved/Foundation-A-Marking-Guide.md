### Six-mark best-fit rubric

- **0 marks:** No relevant creditworthy content.
- **1–2 marks:** A few relevant observations or improvements with little explanation; the judgement is weakly supported.
- **3–4 marks:** Some linked use of the graph/osmosis and useful improvements, but the interpretation, justification or evaluation is incomplete.
- **5–6 marks:** A coherent, evidence-based judgement links the zero-change estimate and net water movement to dataset limitations and a workable, controlled, repeated investigation that compares several tissue types under matched conditions and compares their zero-change estimates. Improvements are justified, and the limits of generalising to every plant tissue are explained.

Judge the whole response, select the best-fit level, then choose the lower/upper mark according to completeness and coherence. Do not count six keywords or require every possible control. For 5–6, both the scientific interpretation and justified methodological evaluation need to be developed. A strong answer can use other valid controls. For the top level, the response must develop how tissue-type comparisons under matched conditions address the universal claim; repeats of one tissue alone do not do so. Accept equivalent scientifically valid approaches and wording, including a justified counterexample design using another tissue type. Do not require a set phrase, named tissues, every listed control, or a checklist of points; apply the levels holistically. Merely saying “repeat for accuracy” is less developed than explaining that repeats expose variation and support a mean.

### Illustrative model response

For this celeriac tissue under these conditions, the graph suggests no net mass change at about 0.40 mol/dm³. Below this concentration the tissue gains mass as water enters overall by osmosis through partially permeable cell membranes; above it the tissue loses water overall. This does not establish the same value for every plant tissue. Surface liquid would affect the measured mass, and different soaking times make comparisons unfair. I would use equal-sized cylinders from comparable tissue, equal solution volumes, time and temperature, and consistent gentle blotting before weighing. Repeats and means would improve the evidence, with extra concentrations near 0.40 to refine the estimate. I would also test several different plant tissue types using the same concentration range and matched conditions, repeating each type and comparing their estimated zero-change concentrations. Different estimates would refute the claim; similar estimates for the tested types would still not prove it for every plant tissue. No net change does not mean water molecules stop moving.

Foundation graph estimate: approximately 0.40 mol/dm³. Higher: approximately 0.32 mol/dm³; allow a justified graphical estimate from 0.31 to 0.33 on the supplied graph. These are estimates, not required spurious exactness. Percent changes in 7a are from separate pilot batches and are not answered by the 7b graph.
